Saudi pharmacy law, with the article it comes from

Law questions are where candidates lose marks to confident misinformation. Every statement below names the law and the article it comes from, read in September 2026. The last section is the one to read twice: five things taught in prep files that the primary sources do not support.

Who regulates what

Three bodies, and questions often turn on telling them apart.

  • SFDA (الهيئة العامة للغذاء والدواء), established by Council of Ministers resolution no. (1) of 07/01/1424 H. For a pharmacist: drug registration and pricing, the registered drug list, the electronic narcotics system that every controlled dispensing in a private therapeutic institution is entered into, and the rule that a substituted alternative must itself be registered with SFDA.
  • Ministry of Health, under the Health Law (النظام الصحي), Royal Decree M/11 of 23/3/1423 H, Article 5: responsible for providing health care, and for the instructions on preventing, reporting and containing communicable and quarantinable diseases.
  • SCFHS (الهيئة السعودية للتخصصات الصحية), which registers and classifies practitioners. Note carefully what it does not do. Under Article 2 of the Law of Practising Healthcare Professions the licence to practise is issued by the Ministry, and registration with SCFHS is one of the conditions of that licence, not the licence itself. A question that offers SCFHS as the licensing body is testing exactly this. Article 2(c) adds the case worth knowing: appointment to a health profession post in a government body counts as the licence to practise within that body, provided SCFHS registration precedes it.

Dispensing without a prescription

The Law of Practising Healthcare Professions, Article 23(a)(2), prohibits a pharmacist from dispensing any medicine except on a prescription issued by a physician licensed to practise in the Kingdom, and excepts the medicines the Ministry determines. The executive regulations, clause 2-23(c), identify that exception as the medicines in the Saudi Guide for Non Prescription Medicines (الدليل السعودي للأدوية اللاوصفية).

So the general rule is not a list of banned drugs. It is the reverse: everything needs a prescription unless the Ministry has put it on the non prescription guide.

Antibiotics

There is no separate antibiotics statute. The rule is Article 23(a)(2) applied to antibiotics by a Ministry decision, enforced from 2018. MOH's own announcement of 6 May 2018 describes the start of a continuous inspection campaign in all regions to verify that antibiotics are not dispensed except on a prescription, applying to every pharmacy in the Kingdom, with the physician recording refill information where needed and the patient keeping the prescription.

Penalties are those of Article 28 of the same Law: imprisonment of up to six months and a fine of up to SAR 100,000, or one of the two penalties, and without prejudice to any more severe penalty in another law. Cancellation of the licence is not an Article 28 penalty, and an option that lists it among them is testing that distinction. Cancellation is disciplinary, under Articles 31 and 32, where the penalties are a warning, a fine of up to SAR 10,000, and cancellation of the licence with removal of the name from the register, after which a new application may not be submitted for at least two years. Disciplinary liability runs in addition to criminal and civil liability, not instead of it.

Controlled substances

Source: the SFDA compendium Procedures and Controls for Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances (إجراءات وضوابط المخدرات والمؤثرات العقلية), published 21 November 2021 and last updated 18 July 2024, 186 pages, Arabic and English side by side.

The schedules are four, not five

Article 1 of the Law of Combating Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances defines four annexed schedules: (I) narcotic drugs, (II) psychotropic substances, (III) chemical precursors, (IV) plants. There is no Saudi equivalent of the United States Schedule I to V, and a question that offers one is testing whether you imported American law.

How long a prescription may cover

  • Narcotics for clinic patients and outpatients: a period not exceeding sixty days.
  • Narcotics in emergencies: not more than three days, after which the patient is referred to a physician authorised to prescribe for longer.
  • Psychotropics for clinic and outpatients: not exceeding thirty days, except for epilepsy, Parkinson's disease, attention deficit and hyperactivity disorder, peripheral and central neuropathic pain and fibromyalgia, where a consultant, senior registrar or registrar may prescribe up to ninety days.
  • Psychotropics in emergencies: not more than three days. Inpatients: not more than seven days, repeatable if needed.

How long the prescription stays valid

From the Implementing Regulations, Article 20, and the Procedures and Controls, Article 10(3)(a)(v):

  • One day only in emergency cases.
  • Seven days for clinic patients and patients discharged from hospital.
  • Forty eight hours for an inpatient medical order sheet, from issue.

The form itself is prescribed too: one medication only per prescription, an original headed in red with مادة مقيدة and two copies marked غير قابل للصرف, the original kept in the pharmacy. The diagnosis must be written on the prescription according to the international classification.

Five things commonly taught that the sources do not support

These appear in circulated prep files. We could not confirm any of them from a primary source, so no Saydana question depends on any of them, and you should treat a question that turns on one as unreliable rather than as something you failed to memorise.

  1. "The antibiotic decision is number X, dated Y." The year, the authority and the penalties are solid. MOH's own pages give no decision number and no commencement date, and the May 2018 announcement describes enforcing a decision already in force.
  2. "A prescription is valid for N days." The executive regulations we read set no general validity period; they require only the Ministry's conditions and the professionally recognised ones. The only published periods we could find are the controlled substance ones above, so treat a general number as unsourced rather than as a rule you missed.
  3. "Forty CPD hours every two years." The forty hours and the two year registration period come from two different SCFHS pages. No single source states the combination.
  4. "The requirements for moving from pharmacist to senior pharmacist are ..." The three grades (صيدلي، صيدلي أول، استشاري صيدلة) come from Article 16 of the SCFHS executive rules, but that document is marked 1438 H and the newer bylaws are a scan whose text layer could not be read. Use the grades; do not trust a question on the requirements between them.
  5. "Wasfaty does X" or "Seha does Y." Wasfaty is verified only from MOH statements of 2021 and 2022. In 2026 "Seha" refers to Seha Virtual Hospital, while the patient platform is Sehhaty. A question depending on the current name, scope or status of a national platform is a question about this year's branding, not about pharmacy.

Corrected on 23 September 2026. An earlier version of this page listed "BCG is given at birth" among the unsettled items. That was wrong, and the correction matters because the answer is testable: BCG is given at six months. The Ministry of Health postponed BCG from birth to six months of age in August 2019. Two sources agree: the published MOH national immunisation schedule shows BCG in the six months column with birth, two months and four months empty, and the change and its date are stated in the peer reviewed literature (Frontiers in Immunology, 2025, PMC12098544). Learn six months.

How to use this

Learn the four schedules, Article 23(a)(2) with its non prescription exception, the Article 28 penalties, and the controlled substance durations and validities. Those are the law facts that are both testable and verifiable. For everything in the list above, know that it is contested, and do not lose time memorising a number that no published source supports.
